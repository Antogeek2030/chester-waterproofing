# **The Complete Leopard Gecko Care Guide**

Native to arid and semi-arid regions, leopard geckos make accommodating pets today. 

They're generally hardier than many other commonly kept reptiles, having adapted well to captive life over generations of breeding. 

This guide walks [through everything you need to know to keep a leopard gecko healthy and thriving](https://chesterwaterproofingspecialists.com/blog/helooooo), from setting up their enclosure to spotting the early signs of illness. 

But before we dive into the nitty-gritties, here's a quick checklist to get you oriented.

## **Quick Checklist**

- **Enclosure:** minimum 20-gallon long for one adult, secure lid, multiple hides, including a humid hide
- **Temperatures:** warm side 88–92°F surface, cool side 70–77°F, thermostat-controlled heat source
- **Diet:** gut-loaded insects dusted with calcium (and occasional multivitamin), fresh water always available
- **Humidity:** 30–40% ambient, higher in humid hide only
- **Handling:** support the body, avoid the tail, keep sessions positive
- **Health:** monitor weight, appetite, and shedding; seek reptile vet care when needed

**1. Leopard Gecko Tank Setup**

A proper enclosure is the foundation of good leopard gecko care. Juveniles can start in smaller setups, 10–15 gallons, but they shouldn't stay there for long. As they grow, their tank needs to grow with them. 

By adulthood, a single leopard gecko needs at least a 20-gallon long tank (roughly 30 x 12 x 12 inches), and that's genuinely a minimum.

Bigger is always better. Many keepers recommend 40-gallon setups, since more floor space allows for a more gradual temperature gradient and gives your gecko the room it needs to move, hunt, and explore the way it would in the wild.

When it comes to materials, you have flexibility. Glass aquariums, PVC enclosures, and large plastic storage tubs all work well, provided they are secure, escape-proof lid and well ventilated. 

Screen tops are fine too, though they may require some adjustments to keep humidity and temperature from slipping away too easily.

Once you've settled on an enclosure, look into what goes inside the gecko’s habitat:

- **Hides**: Add a warm hide on the heated side to support digestion and basking, a cool hide on the opposite side to give your gecko somewhere to retreat and de-stress, and a humid hide lined with damp moss or paper towel to offer support during shedding.
- **A shallow water dish**. It should be arge enough for your gecko to soak if it wants to, but shallow enough that drowning isn't a concern.
- **Optional climbing or resting items.** Low branches, cork bark, or slate pieces aren't strictly necessary, but they add variety and exercise, give your gecko more territory to explore, and can meaningfully reduce stress, especially for more skittish individuals who benefit from having extra places to retreat.

Substrate choice matters just as much as anything above it, and it's an area where caution pays off. 

For beginners, paper towels or reptile carpet are the safest choices, since they eliminate any risk of impaction, a dangerous blockage that occurs when a gecko accidentally swallows loose substrate while hunting. 

Loose substrates such as sand, calcium sand, or gravel carry that same impaction risk if swallowed and are best avoided, especially with young geckos still learning to hunt cleanly. 

Once you're more experienced and your gecko has developed reliably good feeding habits, you can resort to fine, clump-forming substrates or textured tile that offer a more natural look while remaining a safe choice for adults.

## **2. Heating & Lighting**

Leopard geckos are ectothermic, meaning they rely on external heat sources to regulate their body temperature and because of this, a proper thermal gradient is the backbone of a healthy enclosure. 

Aim for:

- **Warm side surface temperature:** An under-tank heat mat (UTH) placed beneath one side of the enclosure is the standard setup, creating a warm zone of about 88–92°F on the belly-heat side where your gecko can soak up the heat it needs to stay active.
- **Cool side:** The opposite side of the tank should stay cooler, around 75°F, giving your gecko a gradient to move across as needed. This back-and-forth movement is exactly how they self-regulate their body temperature throughout the day.

The most reliable heating method is an under-tank heater (UTH) or heat mat placed under one-third to one-half of the enclosure and controlled by a quality thermostat. This pairing matters a lot. Heat mats without a thermostat can overheat and cause severe burns, since geckos don't always move away from surfaces that are too hot, even when they're being harmed.

To avoid killing your gecko, monitor temperatures with digital probe thermometers or an infrared temperature gun. Place one probe on the warm-side surface and another on the cool side so you're always working from real readings rather than assumptions.

Lighting, on the other hand, is much simpler. Because leopard geckos are nocturnal, they don't need bright basking lights or high-output UVB the way many diurnal lizards do. 

That said, low-level UVB provides positive results for calcium metabolism and overall health, making it a worthwhile addition even if it isn't strictly required. 

Beyond that, a simple 12-hour day/night cycle using room lighting or a low-wattage LED is sufficient to keep their internal clock on track.

## **3. Feeding**

Leopard geckos are insectivores. Their diet consists of live insects. Variety matters here. Common feeders include:

- Dubia roaches. Makes an excellent staple. 
- Crickets
- Occasional treats like waxworms or hornworms. These are fatty and should be limited. Treats such as waxworms, superworms, or hornworms can be offered occasionally for variety or to help underweight animals gain condition, but they are high in fat and should not form the bulk of the diet.
- Black soldier fly larvae. It’s nutritious and soft-bodied. 
- Mealworms. Give it in moderation due to higher chitin content.

Supplementation is critical. Dust most feedings with a calcium powder, with vitamin D3 if no UVB is provided, and without D3 if UVB is used. Add a reptile multivitamin once or twice per month. Proper gut-loading of feeder insects with nutritious vegetables and commercial gut-load products 24–48 hours before feeding significantly improves the nutritional value of the meal.

Offer an amount the gecko can consume in about 10–15 minutes, roughly insects no larger than the width of the gecko’s head. Remove any uneaten insects after feeding to prevent stress or injury to the gecko.

That said, feeding frequency depends on age. Hatchlings and juveniles under a year old should be fed daily, while adults typically do well on two to three times per week. Remove uneaten live insects after feeding to prevent them from stressing or nibbling on your gecko overnight.

Besides food, always provide a shallow dish of fresh, clean water. Some geckos will drink from it regularly; others obtain most of their moisture from food.

## **4. Humidity & Shedding**

Leopard geckos hail from arid, rocky terrain, so their enclosure should mimic that dryness. Ambient humidity sitting around 30–40% is the sweet spot. 

The one exception is the humid hide, a small dedicated shelter kept much moister, around 70–80%, which gives geckos a place to retreat when shedding time rolls around. 

Shedding itself is a completely natural process, and you shouldn't be alarmed to see your gecko eating its own discarded skin. It's instinctive behavior, not a cause for concern. 

A healthy shed typically comes off in large, mostly intact pieces with minimal fuss. The real trouble starts when bits of skin get left behind, particularly around the toes and the tip of the tail. This "retained shed" if left unaddressed, can tighten around a toe like a tiny tourniquet, slowly cutting off circulation and, in severe cases, causing the gecko to lose the digit entirely. 

The good news is that catching it early makes treatment simple. Get a warm soak to soften the stuck skin, paired with easier access to the humid hide, is enough to clear things up and get your gecko's shed cycle back on track.

## **5. Handling**

Once your leopard gecko has settled into its new home, after one to two weeks of minimal disturbance beyond feeding and spot-cleaning, it's ready for its first gentle introduction to handling. 

Approach slowly. Rather than scooping your gecko up or gripping it, allow it to walk from hand to hand at its own pace, with your palms always there to support its entire body. This slow, hands-as-a-pathway approach helps your gecko feel secure, which makes all the difference in how quickly it warms up to you. 

One rule matters above all others: never pick your gecko up by the tail. Like many lizards, leopard geckos can shed their tails as a defense mechanism known as autotomy when they feel threatened. Yes, the tail does grow back, but it returns looking different. The experience is stressful for the animal. 

Keep your first sessions short, just a few minutes at a time, and let your gecko's comfort level guide how quickly you extend that duration. 

Throughout each session, stay attentive to your gecko's body language. Watch for tail-waving, squeaking, or repeated attempts to scurry away. These are signs it's had enough, and respecting those cues early on builds the kind of trust that makes a calm gecko in the long run.

**6. Health Issues**

A healthy leopard gecko tells you a lot just by how it looks and acts. It has clear eyes, a thick tail, smooth skin, a regular appetite, and well-formed stools all point to an animal that's thriving. 

Since these geckos are crepuscular and nocturnal, don't be alarmed by daytime stillness. Expect most of their activity to pick up at dusk and continue through the night.

Still, even attentive keepers should stay alert, because leopard geckos are notoriously good at masking illness until a problem has progressed. 

Common health problems include:

- Metabolic bone disease (MBD)
- Impaction from substrate ingestion or oversized prey
- Parasites (internal or external)
- Respiratory infections, often linked to incorrect humidity or temperature.
- Weight loss or anorexia

The tail deserves special attention here, since it acts almost like a built-in health gauge. A thin, shriveled tail signals poor nutrition or an underlying illness brewing beneath. 

Beyond the tail, keep an eye out for other warning signs, especially when they appear in combination:

- Lethargy or reduced activity
- Loss of appetite lasting more than a week or two
- Discharge from the eyes, nose, or mouth
- Diarrhea or abnormal stool
- Visible weight loss

If you notice any of these symptoms, consult an exotic veterinarian. Early intervention makes a real difference in outcomes. 

To stay ahead of problems, schedule annual wellness exams and periodic fecal tests, and always quarantine new animals for at least 30–60 days before introducing them to your collection.

**Conclusion**

Leopard geckos make low-maintenance companions, but that ease of care hinges on meeting a few core needs:

- A secure enclosure with a reliable temperature gradient
- A varied and properly supplemented insect diet
- Appropriate humidity management for shedding
- Regular observation so problems are caught early

Each of these elements works together. A well-regulated enclosure supports healthy digestion. Proper humidity keeps shedding smooth and stress-free, and a nutrient-rich diet only does its job when paired with attentive daily observation. 

Get these fundamentals right and stay consistent with them. The reward is significant. Your leopard gecko can live 15–20 years or more, becoming a solid companion in your home.

So pay close attention to your individual animal's habits and cues, and let that ongoing observation guide how you fine-tune its care over time.
