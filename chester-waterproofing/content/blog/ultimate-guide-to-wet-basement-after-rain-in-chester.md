## **Most Common Causes of Water After Rain**

Water can enter your basement through multiple points. Locating where it appears is the fastest way to identify the cause.

### **1. Poor surface drainage**

Poor surface drainage starts with a clogged gutter. When your gutters fill with leaves and debris, rainwater spills over and pours along the foundation wall. The short downspouts release water a few feet from the house, creating a natural collection bowl that channels every storm's runoff right back to the walls. Over time, repeated saturation softens the ground and increases hydrostatic pressure against the walls, setting the stage for cracks, leaks, and structural movement.

### **2. Foundation cracks and penetrations**

Foundation cracks are direct paths for water to enter the basement. They form as the foundation adjusts to the soil beneath it. This creates hairline openings that widen under hydrostatic pressure during heavy rain. Each crack or gap acts like a sieve, letting moisture seep into your basement.

### **3. Failed sump pumps**

This is another reason your basement could be damp. If your pumps get overwhelmed by heavy rainfall, a power outage, or years of neglect, groundwater will accumulate around the foundation and enter your basement. That’s because it has nothing to redirect it away. 

### **4. Window wells and bulkheads**

An uncovered window well can collect rain that fills faster, overflows and goes straight into your basement. Bulkhead doors carry a similar risk. A worn seal or a drain that's clogged with leaves and debris creates an opening for water to enter basements. 

These issues can escalate quickly during heavier storms, when older sections of the municipal system struggle to keep pace and send sewage backing up through floor drains. 

## **Long-Term Fixes**

Once you handle the immediate water, the next job is ensuring it doesn't happen again.

### **1. Exterior fixes**

Most basement water problems start outside, not in, which means the fix usually does too. 

- Begin by regrading the soil around your foundation so it slopes away from the house. Aim for at least a 6-inch drop over the first 10 feet since flat or inward-sloping ground causes water to pool against your walls. 
- Make sure your downspouts are doing their job. Extend them so runoff discharges 4 to 6 feet away from the foundation instead of dumping it right at the base. 
- Clean your gutters at least twice a year since a clogged or disconnected downspout can undo all your other efforts in one heavy storm. 
- If your property still holds water after these fixes, a French drain can redirect surface and groundwater before it reaches your walls. For homes with below-grade windows. It adds window well covers to keep water and debris out. 
- Use epoxy or polyurethane injection to fix small cracks as they appear. They are a quick fix. Anything wider or spreading requires a professional inspection. 

These steps address the full path water takes to get into a basement, which is exactly why tackling them as a system works so much better than fixing them one at a time.

### **2. Interior Waterproofing:**

An interior drain tile system intercepts water before it reaches the floor. For homes in neighbourhoods with older combined sewer lines, a backwater check valve on the main sewer line prevents storm-driven sewage backups that can turn a wet basement into a hazardous one. 

Applying a vapor barrier to foundation walls further reduces moisture transfer, tackling the persistent dampness that can degrade air quality over time.

If your basement sits near a river or in a low-lying area with a high water table, a full interior/exterior waterproofing system or complete basement encapsulation is a more dependable solution.

But if water still shows up more than once, you spot visible cracks, or you have any significant flooding, an inspection is the smart next step.
